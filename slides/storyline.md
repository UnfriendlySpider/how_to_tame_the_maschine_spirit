## Logline

Steve is a programmer who works alone in a basement and has more ideas than hands, and after work he is tired. Then word gets round that a company rents out virtual workers, the Worker Company, and its catalogue lets him choose each one's attributes. He switches the first one on with a candle lit, incense burning and his fingers crossed. Every fix he accepts, reluctantly and only after the previous stage has hurt him, buys enough trust or growth to expose the next gap. At the end he runs a small company, and the three things that must never fail no longer depend on the model. His look keeps pace: he starts in his own clothes, puts on a hooded robe to switch the first worker on, sheds it piece by piece and ends in his own clothes again, with a mug of tea.

## Telling the story

How to deliver the notes, gathered on 2026-10-09 from guidance on plain language, on stories and on speaking. Since 2026-10-19 the notes are bullets, at the author's request: each slide's intention, its points and at most one line to say word for word, so the story is told in the speaker's own words and never read.

- **The shape.** Each beat runs And, But, Therefore: what Steve has, what goes wrong, what he puts in place (Randy Olson's ABT, https://ncse.ngo/and-therefore-randy-olson-and-art-science-storytelling-part-1). Across the talk it is the story spine: once there was Steve, every evening an idea, one day the catalogue, because of that each problem, until finally the company (Kenn Adams, https://www.kunc.org/2026-06-23/meet-the-creator-of-the-story-spine-an-8-sentence-tool-to-create-and-analyze-stories). The room is the hero who leaves with the tools; Steve is the example.
- **Let the picture land.** Click, look at the picture for a moment, then speak. The five seconds per slide in the timing are for this. Never read out what the picture shows; say what happens. Words on screen that repeat the narration lower what people take away (Mayer, Heiser and Lonn, 2001, https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.93.1.187).
- **Know each intention by heart.** It is what the slide must land, and the notes set it in bold; the points are prompts, and the rest comes in your own words. In the run-through of 2026-10-15 the stumbles came in the middle of long sentences, and in the second, of 2026-10-19, the author told the story freely, so the notes hold less text, not more. Since that evening the points say what to share, never what happens: the picture tells the plot, and you bring the examples, so the notes name no data viewer and no example. "Your example" marks the two slides that need one of yours (2.1, 7.1), "Name it" the Claude Code term, and "Set up for" and "Call back" the threads the story ties. TED's guidance is to stay "in speaking mode, not reading mode" and to write how you talk, not how you write (https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/tedx-organizer-guide/speakers-program/prepare-your-speaker/outline-script).
- **Give Steve a voice.** His lines ("I'd rather write it myself", "Quicker than writing it down", "Everything we decided is in there", "A good model sees through that", "Just this once") are said as him: a little slower, a little dry.
- **The one line, deadpan, then wait.** Say it straight, look at the room, and let the laugh come or not.
- **One lesson sentence per fix slide, slowly.** It is the sentence that says what the mechanism does and what it does not ("nothing enforces it", "it can report, it cannot repair", "whatever the model decided"). In political speeches, contrasts and lists of three are among the devices that most often draw applause (Atkinson's work, https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/100468/3/A_PublishedVersion.pdf); the script uses both on purpose. The fifth run-through closed four fixes with "and this improved everything"; the lesson sentence says more in the same time.
- **The callbacks.** "Usually" (2.3, 4.1, 4.2), the Book of Secrets (1.1, 4.1 to 4.3, 7c.1), the catalogue (1.2, 5.2), the meeting that "will matter in a minute" (6.1, 7.1), the keys (7a, 7b), the candle (1.3, 8.2), "again and again" (2.2, 3.1, 5.1, 7.1, 8.1), the Worker Company (1.2, 1.3, every fix, 7b.1, 7c) and the crate (2.2, 2.3). Lean on each one a little when it returns.
- **Point at the names.** Each robot carries its name above it, as in a game, and the Claude Code term underneath. When you say "this is a hook", point at it; there is no need to read it out. The names keep reminding the room for the rest of the talk, and 8.1 is their legend. When a card comes up, introduce the robot from its rows; the card stays up for about twenty seconds. The threat's card after 7a.1 works the same way. Where a label waits for a click, the notes say so in their second line: click as you name the thing, and it pops up.
- **The two long pauses** come after "You should do it first" (7c.2) and after "a matter of prayer" (8.2). Hold them for two or three seconds; they carry the talk's point.
- **Presenter view.** Open it, enlarge the notes text with its buttons, and keep the timer in sight (Microsoft, https://support.microsoft.com/en-gb/office/what-is-presenter-view-98f31265-9630-41a7-a3f1-9b4736928ee3). Above the rule are the intention, the points and the one line; below it is background for the questions.
- **Rehearse aloud, record, time it.** The timing adds up the seconds planned for each slide. Microsoft's Speaker Coach recommends 100 to 165 words per minute, and recorded lectures run at about 145 (Wingrove 2017, https://research.chalmers.se/en/publication/547664). The plan comes to about 21:10 with the four cards and the goodbye, seventy seconds over the 20-minute slot, so the stopwatch decides what to cut. Trust it over the plan, and slow down rather than add words.
